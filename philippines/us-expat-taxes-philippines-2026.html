A Different Kind of American Expat Population

The Philippines is unlike every other country in our coverage in one important way: a huge share of "American expats" here are actually Filipino-American dual citizens, balikbayan returnees, or retirees on the Special Resident Retiree's Visa (SRRV), not corporate transferees. That changes the shape of the tax questions people actually ask. This guide covers the FEIE, the US-Philippines tax treaty (which exists, unlike some Gulf postings) but the missing Totalization Agreement (which doesn't), dual-citizen filing obligations, SRRV retirement planning, and the 40% foreign ownership rule on condos.

Quick Overview: Philippines and US Tax Obligations

The Basic Conflict: The Philippines taxes residents on a progressive scale from 0% (income under PHP 250,000) up to 35% (above PHP 8,000,000, roughly $142,000 USD) under the TRAIN Law. For most American expats at typical salary levels, the effective Philippine rate stays well below the top US federal bracket, meaning the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion, not the Foreign Tax Credit, is usually the primary planning tool here, the reverse of the calculus in a high-tax country like Australia.

Philippines today: A calendar-year BIR (Bureau of Internal Revenue) filing system with returns due April 15 (extended to May 15, 2026 for 2025 income under BIR RMC No. 30-2026), a real bilateral US tax treaty dating to 1982, but no Totalization Agreement, and one of the most accessible retirement visa programs in Southeast Asia (the SRRV).

United States: File Form 1040 by April 15 (automatic extension to June 15 for expats). The FEIE (Form 2555) shields up to $132,900 of earned income for 2026, covering most salaries here entirely. FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) applies once combined foreign accounts exceed $10,000, and FATCA (Form 8938) applies above higher thresholds.

Day-to-Day Realities of Living in the Philippines

Several features of expat life in the Philippines carry direct tax and compliance consequences.

Land Ownership Is Off Limits

Foreigners cannot own land under the 1987 Constitution, regardless of visa status or years of residence. Condominium units are the workaround, but only up to a 40% foreign-ownership cap per building, see our dedicated page for the mechanics.

A Large Dual-Citizen and Balikbayan Population

Many Americans living in the Philippines are Filipino-American dual citizens or former Filipino citizens returning under the Balikbayan program. Dual citizenship doesn't reduce US filing obligations, citizenship-based taxation applies regardless of a second passport.

A Retiree-Heavy Expat Base

The SRRV program (deposit-based, no minimum age exemption route required for those 50+) has made the Philippines one of the most popular retirement destinations for Americans in Asia, which shapes much of the demand for Social Security and drawdown planning covered later in this guide.

Primary Strategy

FEIE Is Usually Your Main Lever

Because Philippine tax brackets only reach 35% above roughly $142,000 USD of annual income, most American salaries here fall well under that threshold, meaning your Philippine effective rate is typically lower than what you'd owe the IRS on the same income. The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555) shields up to $132,900 of earned income for 2026, covering most expat salaries entirely.

High earners above the FEIE cap, or those with significant Philippine-sourced investment income, should model the Foreign Tax Credit as a supplement, but for the typical teacher, remote worker, or retiree, the FEIE alone usually eliminates most or all US tax liability on Philippine wages.

Structural Gap

A Real Treaty, But No Totalization Agreement

Unlike Oman or Saudi Arabia, the US and Philippines have had a real income tax treaty since 1982. But the Philippines is not among the roughly 30 countries with a US Totalization Agreement, so self-employed Americans here owe the full 15.3% US self-employment tax on net earnings with no offset, the same structural gap as a Gulf posting, just paired with an actual treaty instead of none at all.

  • ✓ The tax treaty helps with double taxation on specific income categories, it does not touch Social Security/self-employment tax
  • ✓ Freelancers and remote contractors should budget for the full 15.3% SE tax rather than assuming treaty protection
  • ✓ A properly structured local corporation can sometimes change this calculus, worth a specialist review

Six Issues That Catch US Expats in the Philippines Off Guard

1. Dual Citizenship Doesn't Reduce US Filing Obligations

A large share of "American expats" here are Filipino-American dual citizens. Citizenship-based taxation applies regardless. See our dedicated page: Dual Citizens & Balikbayan.

2. No Totalization Agreement

Self-employed Americans owe the full 15.3% self-employment tax with no Totalization offset, despite the tax treaty existing for income tax purposes.

3. Cannot Own Land

Land ownership is constitutionally restricted to Filipino citizens. Condominium units are the primary path to real property for foreigners, capped at 40% foreign ownership per building.

4. FBAR and FATCA on Philippine Bank Accounts

Combined Philippine bank and investment account balances over $10,000 at any point in the year trigger FBAR (FinCEN Form 114), with FATCA (Form 8938) applying at higher thresholds.

5. SRRV Deposit and Retirement Fund Reporting

The SRRV's required deposit (typically $20,000-$50,000, held in a Philippine bank) itself counts toward your FBAR and FATCA thresholds, and is frequently overlooked since it's framed as a visa requirement rather than an investment account.

6. The New Digital Nomad Visa's Uncertain US Eligibility

Executive Order 86's 2026 digital nomad visa requires reciprocity, the applicant's home country must offer Filipinos an equivalent visa. As of this writing, US eligibility hasn't been officially confirmed, so most American remote workers still rely on tourist visa extensions. See our dedicated Digital Nomad Visa guide.

FAQ: US Expat Taxes in the Philippines 2026

Q: I'm a dual citizen born in the Philippines, do I still owe US tax? A: Yes. Citizenship-based taxation applies to all US citizens regardless of a second passport or birthplace.

Q: Does the US-Philippines tax treaty stop double taxation entirely? A: It helps with specific income categories, but a savings clause preserves US taxation of citizens, most of your relief still comes from the FEIE or FTC under domestic law.

Q: Can I buy a house in the Philippines? A: Not land directly, foreigners cannot own land, but a condominium unit is available subject to the 40% foreign ownership cap per building.

Q: Is the Philippines a good retirement destination for Americans? A: Many find it so, largely due to the accessible SRRV visa program, but plan the deposit and Social Security interaction with a specialist before committing.

Q: What's the FBAR threshold, and does my SRRV deposit count? A: $10,000 aggregate across all foreign accounts, and yes, your SRRV deposit held in a Philippine bank counts toward that threshold.
